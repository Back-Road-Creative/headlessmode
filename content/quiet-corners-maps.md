## What it does

Quiet Corners Field Maps turns a rectangle you draw, or one or more USGS 7.5-minute quadrangles you pick, into a topographic map of that area, printed on ordinary Letter paper. The sheets are indexed so they tape together edge to edge, and every sheet carries the retrieval date of each source it was built from. Forest Service road-use seasons are drawn over the USGS Topo base. The PDF is assembled in your browser: no upload, no account, no server of ours, though the map services you load tiles from still see your requests ([privacy page](/privacy/)). It is at proof stage, so read [Status](#status) before you rely on a printed sheet.

[Open field maps](/quiet-corners/#dashboard)

## Choose an area

You build a print set on the [Quiet Corners](/quiet-corners/) map itself, under “Print field maps of this area” — the same map that shows the county ranking, because there is only one. Once an area is drawn, a strip above the map holds it: **Edit** goes back to drawing, **Undo** restores the area you just replaced (once — a second Undo does not go further back), and **Clear print area** removes it outright, taking the saved recipe with it — and, for an area that came from a shared link, the link's own area settings in the address bar. Clear leaves the map where it is and every layer as you had it, and it holds even if you reload straight away or the saved map setup was slow to read. Paper orientation, sheet overlap, the UTM grid and print quality live under **Options**, a disclosure closed by default at the foot of the print builder, and default to Letter portrait, 0.5 in overlap, UTM 1 km grid and 300 dpi unless you open it and change them.

Picked quadrangles are listed under the area controls, each with a Remove button and a note of what removing it saves, and clicking a quadrangle on the map toggles it. This page is where the sources, the printing instructions and the calibration procedure are written down; the [privacy page](/privacy/) says what the map services receive.

Text on the map itself is sized to be read outdoors on a phone: the Share view, Reset view, Use my location, starting-set and 3D buttons are 14px, the map credit line is 12px and wraps onto a second line rather than shrinking when it gets longer, the scale bar and hover labels are 13px, print-sheet ids are 12px, and the map key is 13px. A key row for something the area does not contain, or whose state is unknown, is shown in a muted grey at 80% strength rather than faded to 55%, so it stays readable (at least 4.5:1 against the panel) and still reads as the odd one out.

## Starting sets

Above the layer switches are seven buttons, each a named set of those same switches for one kind of outing. A button is a shortcut, never a mode: it ticks and unticks the boxes below and moves the two choices above them, you can see exactly which ones it moved, and every one of them is still yours to change afterwards. No layer reaches the map any other way. Before you press one, its own screen-reader description names which switches it would actually move — a switch already set the way the preset wants is left off that list, and the base map is named only when yours disagrees with it.

- **Map only** — every overlay off, on the topo base, except **Road names** and **Named places & landmarks**. Those two open on, and this preset leaves them as they are. The ground itself, with only road and place names over it.
- **Trails & terrain** — hiking trails, named places, and the hillshade relief surface, on the topo base. It answers what the ground does around a trail: which way it climbs, and what the named summits, gaps and ridges nearby are called.
- **Land access** — federal land designations, MVUM road and trail designations, Forest Service and BLM recreation sites, and the state land families (game lands, state parks, other state-managed areas) your drawn area covers. It answers which public and state-managed land reaches this ground and how to get onto it. State land rows only fill in once an area is drawn — draw one first if a switch reads "unavailable."
- **Compare counties** — county boundaries and the [Quiet Corners county ranking](/quiet-corners/), the same context surface the county-score row on the dashboard turns on. It answers how one county compares to the ones around it. The ranking's colour shading appears only while that row is on; with it off the dashboard map draws the county outlines alone, so the base map under them is not tinted.
- **Night sky** — the night light emissions surface, with the boundary lines out of the way and no base map under it. It answers whether tonight is worth the drive from here, and how far out you would have to go. Aurora and air quality are not switched on by this set; pick either one yourself under **Land, sky & light context**. Precipitation radar is deliberately *not* in this set: it reports what the beam met aloft in the minutes just past, which is not cloud tonight — the hourly conditions card is what answers the hours ahead — and it is a second full-screen picture over the first, which is the clutter limit below.
- **Public land** — national parks, forests, wilderness and refuges; Forest Service land ownership parcel by parcel; MVUM road and trail designations; hiking trails; Forest Service and BLM recreation sites; on the topo base. It answers where you may legally go and what is there when you arrive. A boundary still says nothing about access — see the section below.
- **Loud or quiet** — the BTS transportation noise surface with **Rail lines** and **Airports** over it, plus named places, on the topo base. Road names open on and this preset leaves them on. It answers whether a place is near the rail lines and airports that make it loud, or away from them. The noise picture is a model of an average day in 2020; the rail lines and airports are inventories of where those sources are, and none of the three measures how loud your spot is.

Two buttons on the line under the presets that counts the layers that are on, **Undo** and **Reset**, both start out disabled. Undo restores exactly the switches the last preset you pressed moved, once, and is disabled again afterwards — a second Undo does not go further back. Reset becomes enabled after you change a layer switch, the base map or the context surface by hand, or after a preset moves something. Changing the highlight or the hillshade light does not enable it. To put those two back, use **Clear highlight** and **Reset light**.

Once Reset is enabled, pressing it puts back every layer switch (the ones that open on, Road names and Named places & landmarks, go back on; all others go off), the base map (USGS Topo), the context surface (No context surface), the highlight, and the hillshade light.

Reset leaves alone your drawn area, the print recipe, a saved pin, and **Map label size** and **Map label crowding**.

Both buttons say what they did on the status line below the map, the same line every other action here uses.

**What these sets are held to.** Two of the three things worth measuring here are enforced by a test rather than by good intentions, and a set that exceeded either could not be built at all. The third, task completion, is not mechanical: it is watched rather than asserted, and what the code holds each set to is that it states the question it claims to answer.

- **Load size.** Each layer switched on is one query of its own against its service, and a query pages up to twenty requests. The public-land set is six layers, a worst case of 120 requests for one settled view; the night set asks the vector services for nothing at all, because its surface is a picture handed straight to the map. Six is the ceiling until a wider set has been measured.
- **Clutter.** At most one continuous-colour surface at a time, and that is enforced rather than promised: in **Land, sky & light context** the surfaces that fill the whole view as a picture — the steepness of the ground, night lights, radar, land cover, tree canopy, the [Quiet Corners county ranking](/quiet-corners/) — are a choice of one, or **No context surface**, exactly like the base map above them. It used to be four independent checkboxes, so a reader could stack four filled surfaces over each other and nothing stopped them; the light-emissions surface is drawn at 80% and the radar at 55%, and those two alone leave under a tenth of whatever is beneath them showing. What is drawn as outlines or shading on top and hides nothing beneath it — wildfire perimeters, air quality, smoke, flood zones, the aurora and wildlife records — is not part of that choice: each is its own checkbox under the surfaces and stays on whichever surface you pick, so fire perimeters and air quality can be on together, and with night lights as well. A saved view or shared link from before the split that names one of them as its surface still turns it on. **No context surface** is where the panel starts, and it stays a row of its own so turning them all off again is always one click. On the map key, the public-land set draws nineteen distinct marks and the night set draws one. The county ranking is the one surface here with no service behind it at all — it is the dashboard's own local data, drawn on screen only, and it is never in the printable-vector fetch loop and never on the printed key: a screen-only layer switched on for the screen used to still be listed as a source on the sheet that never carried it, and the credit list is now built from what actually reaches paper rather than from what the reader had switched on.

## Finding and checking a layer

The layer list grows with the map, so above it is a search box and an "Only layers that are on" toggle — the search narrows the rows shown by name or description as you type, the toggle hides everything not switched on, and neither ever changes a switch itself: turn off the search or the toggle and every row, on or off, is exactly as you left it. A line above the list states how many layers are on and how many are still loading. Each federal or state layer carries a small word next to its name — **off**, **loading**, **ready**, **empty** (fetched, nothing here), **partial** (the service stopped part way through), **unavailable**, or **zoom in** (too far out for this map to ask yet) — and its own "About this layer" details: where the data comes from, its licence and coverage caveats, and whether it has been fetched for the area drawn or not yet. A layer reading unavailable or partial gets a Retry button that asks that one service again, without re-fetching anything else or moving the map; unticking a layer's switch is Clear — it removes the layer's lines and its mark from the map key immediately, and the map itself does not move.

## Finding a place

The finder is on the map itself, not in the Layers panel. Press **Search places** to open its box. As you type, it matches the towns Quiet Corners scores, with no request to any service. Results are grouped as **Towns**, **Geographic names** and **Coordinates**. A pair of coordinates is read as a point on the map. To look up a name in the USGS place-name record, press **Search**, or press Enter with no suggestion highlighted. That is the only time the finder asks the USGS, and it never does so as you type. If the USGS cannot be reached, the finder says so and shows the matched towns only.

## Highlight

Two buttons in the Layers panel, **Highlight trails** and **Highlight boundaries**, thicken the lines of one drawn layer and dim everything else on the screen; **Clear highlight** restores exactly what was on screen before, never an approximation of it. A summary line states in words whatever the screen currently differs from the default view, and the map key dims and brightens its swatches the same way the map itself does, from the same state. None of this is a mode: switching a layer on or off, or drawing a new area, works exactly as it did before a highlight was ever pressed.

**Advanced**, closed by default under the buttons, exposes the same handful of knobs by hand: how much to dim everything not highlighted, how heavy the drawn lines are, how solid a filled polygon is, and a menu to spotlight any one layer directly rather than only trails or boundaries. Touching any of these releases a Highlight-button press back to a plain setting — there is no state where a highlight and a manual edit disagree about what is on screen.

**Map appearance**, closed by default under the map key, holds two things. **Map labels** holds the two label controls, explained under Names & landmarks below. The hillshade light controls — light azimuth and altitude, **Sun now** and **Reset light** — only mean something while **Hillshade (adjustable light)** is the chosen context surface, so they show only then; the rest of the time Map appearance says where to turn that surface on.

None of this reaches the printed sheet. The PDF is built from the print recipe alone — area, paper, scale, overlap, grid and the layers you checked — and the emphasis state above is never one of its fields; asking for it is refused outright rather than silently ignored. A screen highlighted for reading is not a page marked up for printing, and the sheet's credits list is built the same way, from what was actually fetched for it, not from anything a highlight touched.

Three settings your device or browser may already be asking for are honoured here specifically because dimming is designed to *reduce* contrast for everything but the highlight, which a reader who has turned up contrast is asking never to happen: **more contrast** and Windows' **forced colors** both cancel every dimmed opacity outright rather than trying to draw a softer version of it, and a highlighted button still marks itself without relying on colour alone. The reduced-motion setting costs nothing extra here, because a highlight or a clear takes effect immediately rather than fading in.

## Terrain in 3D

Press **3D** on the map to open a separate relief view of the area on screen. It shows the shape of the ground, built from a mixed-source elevation tile set (see the Source decision record below), with the USGS topo base and a hillshade on top. It shows terrain, not analysis. It does not compute shadows, line of sight or where you can walk.

Inside the panel:

- **North up / reset view** returns to the view the panel opened on, pointing north and looking straight down.
- **Tilt up** and **Tilt down** change the viewing angle, 10 degrees at a time, between flat and 60 degrees.
- The exaggeration checkbox (named **Exaggerated terrain (1.5×)** for screen readers) steepens the relief from the default 1× to 1.5×. Until you tick it, its visible label reads “Exaggeration: 1× (default) / 1.5× — exaggerated”. Once ticked, the label reads “Terrain exaggerated 1.5× (not true scale)” so the panel says the relief is steepened, and it goes back when you untick the box. Judge how steep the land really is from the unticked view.
- **Close** shuts the panel and frees everything it loaded.

What it draws from the 2D map: the layers you switched on that are lines or outlines are draped over the relief in the same colours. That means trails, MVUM roads and trails, recreation sites, and land and county boundaries. Named places are lettered on the relief at your **Map label size** and **Map label crowding**, and a name behind a ridge is left off rather than shown through the hill. While the Trails layer is on, each named trail is lettered on the relief too, on the part of it that is in view and not hidden by a ridge, and it shares the same room-making as the place names, so on a crowded view some trail names are left off until you zoom in.

What it does not draw: **Road names** stay on the flat map, and no road names are lettered in 3D. Camera layers that are on stand on the relief as pins in their flat-map colours. The raster surfaces (hillshade light, contour lines, modelled snow depth, slope, land cover, tree canopy, night lights, radar, aurora, air quality, wildfire, avalanche danger, wildlife) cannot be draped. A list headed **Layers in this view** names the layers you have on that the panel can show. A line, outline or name layer is marked “shown”, or “loading in 2D first” if the flat map has not fetched it yet, and a raster surface is marked “Available in 2D”. Camera layers that are on are listed as “shown”. The list leaves out the reading rows, the extra sheets and the base map. It can still list **Road names** as “shown”, although the 3D view draws none.

Nothing for this view loads until you press 3D: no rendering library, no elevation tiles, no extra base tiles. The panel needs WebGL. With WebGL off, you get a message in place of the panel and the 2D map is unchanged.

## Print

How to print the sheets at the right size, what the printed sheet carries, and how to check your printer.

### Printing instructions

Print at Actual size (100%) and turn off any Fit or Shrink option. Each sheet carries its own page ID, and inside each grey overlap band the ID of the sheet that adjoins there with an arrow pointing at it. Lap two sheets so the arrows meet, slide them until the corner crosses in the band sit on top of each other, and tape. Tape each row left to right first, then join the rows. A set made from separate quadrangles can skip grid positions: the index page shows which sheets exist, and an edge with no arrow has no neighbour.

### Road names

**Road names** is a layer switch in the same federal group as Named places & landmarks, but **on by default**, because a map with no road names leaves the reader guessing which line is which. It draws lettering along each road from USGS The National Map transportation data, on the screen only: it is never fetched for, or printed on, a sheet, never counts toward the "layers on" line above the list (that line is about what a sheet will carry), and carries its own word beside its name instead. Nothing is asked below zoom 10; interstates and other controlled-access highways appear from zoom 10, secondary highways from 11, connecting roads and 4WD tracks from 12, and local roads from 13. Highways print as route shields (I-26, US 74, NC 107) and other roads by name. After a pan or zoom the map waits 400 ms for you to stop, then asks once for the view you stopped on plus a margin around it, so a small drag inside that margin asks for nothing; a newer view cancels an older request, so the names on screen always match the ground in front of you. How many roads are lettered follows the same **Map label crowding** and **Map label size** controls as the other map names: a road is placed only where its label clears the town and orientation names, the named places and the camera pins already on the map, and where it does not fit it is simply left out, never drawn over them. That holds in either order: Named places drawn after the roads (switched on later, or their answer arriving late) send the road labels back through placement, and they step clear of the new names. The starting-set buttons never touch this switch: Road names is lettering on the map, not a topic, so no button turns it off and no button's preview says it will; **Reset** puts it back on with the rest. If the service had more roads than one request returns, the row says **More roads than shown**; if a request fails it says **Road names did not load** and keeps the names already on screen rather than blanking the map — an unreadable answer is never presented as "no roads here". A name is a published label, not a statement that the road is open, maintained or passable. While the switch is on, the map's credit line names the source; switching it off removes the credit. In 3D, the road names appear over the relief too: open the 3D view with the switch on and it letters the same roads the screen map would for that view, in the same amber on a dark plate, at the same Map label size, turned to lie along each road as you see it (still readable, never upside down) and kept clear of the 3D view's own credit line and of the place names there. It asks the same service the same way, once the camera has held still, and says the same things in its "Layers in this view" list: **Road names: shown**, **Road names did not load**, a plain "no road names found in this view" for an empty answer, or "zoom in to show them" below zoom 10. The 3D view is a snapshot taken when you open it, so to turn road names off there, switch them off on the screen map and open 3D again; the 2D map's labels are untouched by a trip to 3D.

### Names & landmarks

**Named places & landmarks** is a layer switch listed under the same federal group as the base map: summits, gaps, ridges, falls, springs, lakes, populated places and the rest of GNIS's vocabulary, one point per name from USGS's Geographic Names Information System. It is **on by default**, so a reader who touches nothing sees the names; untick it for a plain map, and a saved project that stored it off keeps it off. The USGS Topo base prints its own names, and these decluttered ones may repeat some of them. While it is on, the map asks for whatever area is on screen, with its own per-view pass like Road names: no drawn print area, no zoom floor beyond the lowest zoom a name can draw at (8) and no area ceiling gate it, a pan that stays inside the box already fetched asks for nothing, and a refresh that fails leaves the last labels on the map and says so. Panning or zooming out of that box asks again for the new area, and an answer for a rectangle you have since left is dropped rather than drawn, so the names on screen always match the area in front of you. A search that genuinely finds nothing prints "No named terrain found in this area" — a real answer, not a failure — and a search that did not finish (a timeout, an error) says so distinctly and never draws a silently incomplete set. The request follows the zoom: only the kinds of name that zoom can draw are asked for (places from zoom 8, peaks from 9, and so on), and when an area holds more names than one request carries, the most important kinds are fetched first and drawn, with a note naming what zooming in adds ("Showing places and peaks here -- zoom in for creeks and lakes"). A capped answer still blocks the PDF, like any incomplete one. An answer that came back incomplete is still listed under **Places in this view** below, with a notice that it is incomplete — the map itself labels none of those names until a full answer arrives.

Screen labels declutter automatically as you zoom: only as many names as comfortably fit are lettered, and only a labelled name gets a marker on the map. Every name loaded for the area you're looking at — labelled or not — is listed under **Places in this view** below the layer switch, with filters for the kind of place and a toggle for "Only places not labelled on the map", so nothing the map declutters is ever hidden from you. Tab to a row, or tap it, and it draws that name's marker and opens the same card a map pin opens — its kind, its state and county, its coordinates, the note that GNIS gives one published point per name and not every place the name is used, its approximate terrain elevation fetched from USGS 3DEP once you open the card, and buttons to save the place, copy the coordinates, or copy a shareable link to that place — without moving or re-centering the map. Saved places can also be downloaded as a GPX file, with **Download GPX** under **Saved places**, for a GPS app or another map.

**Forest roads and trails open a card on a tap.** With the forest-road or trail layers on, touching a drawn line (within about 12 px of it for a finger or pen, 6 px for a mouse) opens the same card a click does: its name, what kind of road or trail it is, the maintenance level or trail class the source gives, and the source's credit. Where two or more lines pass within that distance, a short list of them appears first and you pick one; the map never picks for you. Under **Places in this view**, the forest roads and trails in view are listed in their own groups, along with a group named "Roads lettered on the map" for the named roads Road names has actually lettered; choose a row with the keyboard and the same card opens, and closing it with Escape puts you back on that row. The lettering itself is not a touch or tab target: a press on it reaches the map beneath.

Two controls set how names look. Both live in the Layers panel, under **Map appearance** › **Map labels**.

- **Map label size** sets how big every name the map draws prints: Normal (14 px), Large (18 px) or Extra large (24 px). It works on the flat 2D map and in the 3D view, and it covers town names, road names and named places alike.
- **Map label crowding** sets how many named-place labels show at once: Sparse, Standard or More. A numerous kind never crowds a scarcer one out entirely; each kind keeps a share of the slots.

Both are saved with your other display choices and carried in a shared link. Reset does not change them. Every name is lettered on a dark plate in a clear sans-serif, so it stays readable over a county colour, a road or a trail. The sizes follow your browser's own text-size setting, and the room each name gets grows with it. Towns drop the Census's class word, so the map says Sylva, not Sylva town.

**A name on the printed USGS Topo base is not the same thing as a name this layer draws**, and the base map's own text cannot be decluttered the way this layer's labels are: if the topo base already carries a name near a point this layer also names, the two may repeat on paper. See the Named terrain on the sheet section below for exactly how a name reaches a printed sheet.

**A town this layer would name is left off screen if the dashboard has already lettered it.** The same map also carries its own orientation labels — the towns [Quiet Corners](/quiet-corners/) scores, and the larger places you would orient by — and a populated place here sharing one of those labels' name and state is not drawn a second time under this layer's separate rules, allowing for the raw Census suffix a town label can carry (Ashland town still matches Ashland). Screen only: its point is left out of both the map and **Places in this view**, but a printed sheet still draws straight from this layer's own answer and is not decluttered against those labels.

### Named terrain on the sheet

Named summits, gaps, ridges, falls, springs and other GNIS terrain names print as a small shape (one per kind, listed in the key) beside the name in physical type, sized and positioned from the sheet's own scale — never from how far you have zoomed the screen. A name shares the same on-paper space as the road, trail and site labels already there: when a name's text would land on top of something else, it is nudged to the next open spot near its point in a fixed, repeatable order, so the same area prints the same way every time. **The point marker itself never moves** — only its name can be bumped, and if no open spot is found nearby the name is left off and counted in the build record while its marker still prints, so you can see something is there even without a label crowding the sheet. Names print in the same typeface as everything else on the sheet; an accented spelling (Cañón, Étang) prints as GNIS spells it, and only a character that typeface cannot represent at all falls back to `?`, the same fallback every other label on the sheet already uses.

Like the base map and MVUM (see Sources), an interrupted or capped fetch of GNIS names blocks the PDF rather than printing an incomplete set of names with nothing said — see **"Named terrain (GNIS) must load completely before export"** below.

### Printing the key on its own

**Print the key**, under **Options** at the foot of the print builder, hands you a one- or two-page PDF of every symbol the layers you have switched on can draw, with the notices those symbols oblige. It is the same key a built set carries at its back — the same table, the same strokes — so it can be printed and read before a single map sheet is spent, and kept with the sheets afterwards. It lists what the layers *can* draw, not what a particular area contains; the on-page key is what narrows after Check sources. The two keys are deliberately not identical: the on-page key describes the map on your screen, so it drops the UTM grid and the sheet-overlap marks (which exist only on paper) and says of anything the current zoom is too coarse to draw that it appears when you zoom in.

### Calibrating a printer

Before printing a set on a printer you have not used for these sheets, print a calibration sheet: either add `?calibrate=1` to the [Quiet Corners](/quiet-corners/) map's address, or open **Options** at the foot of the print builder and press **Calibration sheet** — both hand you the same one-page PDF instead of the map builder. Print that one sheet at Actual size (100%) with Fit and Shrink off, and it answers the two questions no test can answer from a file:

- **Scale.** Lay a ruler along either 10 cm bar. 9.9 cm instead of 10.0 means the printer prints 1% small, and the map's scale bar will read the same way.
- **Unprintable margin.** The sheet carries nested rectangles 0.25, 0.20, 0.17 and 0.15 in from the paper edge, each labelled. The smallest labelled inset that printed complete on all four sides is the smallest inset that is safe on that printer; the sheets use 0.25 in today. Four corner marks sit on a real sheet's frame corners, so a missing mark means the frame itself is being clipped.

Keep the sheet with the printer. It is good for every future job on that machine.

## What a boundary does not mean

State game lands, parks and other managed areas are drawn as boundaries: a line marks where a designation begins and ends. **It is not a statement that the ground inside is open to you, and it is not a statement that the ground outside is closed.** Seasons, permits, camping, fires and vehicle access are the managing agency's to state, not this map's to infer, and they differ unit by unit. An empty result means the state's service returned nothing for that area, which is not the same as "no public land here". Confirm access and current closures with the managing agency before you travel.

Parcel boundaries are drawn from the counties' own recording and tax maps, at 1:24,000 only. **A parcel line is a record, not a survey**: it is drawn to locate a property for taxation, not measured to establish where its corners are, and a recorded line and a surveyed line disagree routinely. It is not permission to enter. Coverage differs county by county, so a blank area means the county published nothing there rather than that the ground is unowned. **No owner name, mailing address or occupancy field is ever requested** — the catalogue records each state's owner-bearing fields as a refuse-list, and every parcels layer this map offers has had its actual field VALUES audited, not just its field names, because a field named for a category can still hold people: North Dakota's `Ownership` is declared with a nine-value domain and one county had filled it with individual landowners.

Mapped wetlands are neither a boundary nor a landholding, and the map draws them in their own colour for that reason. **A wetland edge is approximate by construction** — it is a line someone drew on an aerial photograph, which is why it is drawn broken rather than solid — and it carries no statement about access, ownership or what any law regulates. Where nothing is drawn, the layer's own row says whether the ground was mapped and holds none or has never been mapped at all; those are different facts and they look identical on the map.

The same sentence is printed on the map key of every set that draws a boundary, because the sheet in your hand is where it matters.

On the screen each boundary family — National Forest, Wilderness, National Park unit, Wildlife Refuge, state game land, state park, other state-managed area — has its own colour and a light fill, so you can see where one begins and tell it from the next; the key beside the map shows the same colours. On paper they are all one blue, told apart by their line pattern, because a printed sheet is a grayscale halftone and a hue would not survive it.

## Lengths and elevations on a card

A forest road or trail card states its length in miles (kilometres beside it), measured along the drawn line on the WGS84 ellipsoid, the same surface GPS uses, not on the flat screen. **The number is the length of what is loaded in this view.** When the whole road or trail is in the area the map fetched, the card says so plainly. When the line reaches the edge of that area, the source answered only in part, the preview drew only some of the lines of that kind, or the source gave the line no route id, the card reads "approximate — only the part loaded in this view" (or says why), because a length is never claimed whole on a guess. A map line is also only as exact as the source drew it: a card length is not a surveyed distance.

A place card's elevation is the ground elevation at that point from the USGS 3D Elevation Program, a surface interpolated between lidar returns. The card names the source and says "vertical reference not stated by source" and "resolution not stated by source" because this site has not recorded either for the elevation samples. The same USGS service answers at whatever grid size a request asks for: its slope layer, measured here, gave a different steepness for one hillside at a 3 m grid than at a 270 m one, so a grid size would be a guess. "Unknown" means the source returned no data there, which is not the same as sea level.

Not measured: raster pixels, distance over the 3D surface (slope, switchbacks), and polygons larger than a hemisphere (measured as their smaller side).

## Status

Proof stage. The engine builds real PDFs; what is outstanding is the physical verification — a ruler-and-tape test on printed sheets — which has to pass before the builder is offered as finished work. Until it does, the builder carries that caveat on its face, wherever it is hosted.

NIFC states these data are dynamic and are not legal documents: nothing here is a closure, an evacuation order, or a statement about any road.

Two things it deliberately does not do. It never prints a Bortle class, a 1-9 rating or any score: upward radiance measured by a satellite and what a human eye sees standing underneath are different quantities, and mapping one onto the other would not be a unit conversion. And it never claims to describe tonight. Its finest detail is zoom 8, which is where the tile set stops — closer in, Leaflet enlarges the same picture and the layer row says so, because an enlarged tile looks exactly like real detail.

- **NOAA GLERL Great Lakes cameras** — [camera page](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/metdata/), a screen-only surface credited "NOAA Great Lakes Environmental Research Laboratory (GLERL)". Only a `--refresh-seeds` run reads the page and HEADs each still (4 at a time, with a pause; a plain GET and HEAD with the build's own User-Agent), keeping a camera whose picture is under 24 hours old (the Muskegon cameras last updated in 2013 drop out). The station list and positions were copied from GLERL's /metdata/ map on 2026-10-05 and are not re-read on a refresh, so a station GLERL adds later is skipped until the table is updated. Rights are `unverified`, credit plus a link to the station's page. [GLERL's site notice](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/home/notice.html) (read 2026-10-05): "Information presented on these pages is considered public information and may be distributed or copied. Use of appropriate byline/photo/image credits is requested." robots.txt disallows only `/incoming/` and a few file types, not `/metdata/`. Pictures are hotlinked from `www.glerl.noaa.gov`, never stored; the cameras show today under the **Research cameras (PhenoCam) switch**, which asks no host for anything when turned on, and opening a pin loads its picture from `www.glerl.noaa.gov`.
- **NOAA NDBC buoy cameras — not included.** The station list (`activestations.xml`) answers, but the picture host `www.ndbc.noaa.gov/buoycam` answers 403 from its CDN to this build's honest User-Agent. The build does not imitate a browser, so NDBC buoy cameras are dropped until NOAA allows the build agent or a public API (a follow-up). A build test fails if any NDBC provider, source, origin or picture host is ever published.

Every source a set is built from is credited by name on its printed map key, derived from what the build actually consumed rather than written by hand.

Tap a recreation site and it also says how far the nearest unpaved road is — "0.7 mi from a mapped crushed aggregate or gravel road (straight-line)". Three things that phrase is careful about: *mapped* means the roads fetched for the area you are looking at and no others; *straight-line* means exactly that, because nothing here calculates a route, a legal right of way or a driving time; and a road whose surface the inventory never stated is not counted as unpaved, so a site with nothing to say says nothing rather than promising gravel nobody looked up.

A road's rating is the Forest Service's record, not a report of current condition: a road can wash out, gate or be logged over without the inventory changing. Where no record matched the route, the road is drawn at its plain width and says nothing extra — an empty rating would read as "the inventory looked and found it ordinary". Where two records disagree about the same route, the map says they disagree rather than picking one.

Honesty rules. MVUM is a designation dataset published forest by forest, not a live passability feed, and not every forest is included. Seasonal dates are shown only when the source supplies them: on screen when you select a route, and on the printed key under **Seasonal routes**, one line per route that reached the sheet. Routes the source called seasonal and gave no dates for are counted there too — listing only the dated ones would read as "these are the seasonal roads here", and you would drive an unlisted one in February on the strength of it. A source that fails to load never disappears silently: it blocks the PDF, or you remove that layer explicitly. Each source prints its own retrieval or dataset date; there is no single composite "as of" date.

## Troubleshooting

**The sheets do not measure true to scale.** The print dialog scaled them. Choose Actual size (100%) and turn off Fit to page, Shrink oversized pages and any borderless option. A test sheet's scale bar is the check: measure it against a ruler before printing the whole set.

**"Check sources before building."** Nothing has been asked of the map services yet. Check sources first; the page will not build a PDF from sources whose state it does not know. Three kinds of layer are never among those sources, so switching one on never holds up the build: a screen-only layer, such as a context surface, which never reaches the PDF; an extra sheet (aerial, shaded relief, route summary), which the build fetches for itself and prints the map without if the service does not answer; and a state land layer that no state in your area publishes, whose switch turns off once Check sources has found which states the area covers. The line under **Build PDF on this device** names only the sources it is waiting on — the base map and each overlay you have switched on — so a layer that is off never shows up there as “not checked yet”.

**Layers do not appear when I switch them on.** Switch the layer on and stop moving the map: once the view settles the page asks for what is on screen and draws it. Two views ask for nothing, on purpose — zoomed out past the level the topo base appears at, and a view covering more ground than one query can honestly answer. Both say so on the page. Building the PDF always re-asks for the printed box itself, so a sheet never draws features fetched for somewhere else.

**Which context surface am I looking at, and when is it from?** Whichever surface is chosen under **Land, sky & light context** — only one can be on at a time — plus any overlay ticked beneath it (fire perimeters, air quality and so on, which can be on together). The map itself carries one line for each, naming it and the time its data refers to; with everything on, that box stops growing at a few lines and scrolls. That time is the source's own and never the moment it was fetched: the aurora surface shows the time it is a **forecast for**, radar and air quality show the time they were **observed**, and the night lights show their **vintage** (a 2016 composite, not tonight). The wildlife records are an aggregate over a span of years rather than a moment — a cell holds records from many different days — so that line says the time is unknown and the row beneath it names the year range the count covers. The steepness surface is arithmetic rather than a reading, and the national elevation model states no date for a given piece of ground, so its line says the time is unknown instead of borrowing the clock. The county ranking names no service and so states its own dashboard vintage rather than a fetch time, the same wording an inventory carries. A source that will not state its time says so on that line rather than being shown as current.

**Can I turn the base map off, or see the aerial or relief on screen?** Yes — under **Base map** in the Layers panel, choose USGS Topo, aerial imagery, shaded relief or no base map at all; the choice is for the screen only, and the tiles are drawn at full strength rather than dimmed. The PDF still prints the topo base on every sheet, with aerial imagery and shaded relief available as companion sheets under **Extra sheets**.

**"USGS Topo must load completely before export."** At least one base-map tile did not come back. The build refuses rather than printing a sheet with a blank patch. Try again — the tile service rate-limits bursts — and if it persists, reduce the area or use 1:48,000, which needs fewer tiles.

**An MVUM layer that reads unavailable, or a coverage that will not reach complete.** The Forest Service service did not answer, or answered partially. MVUM is published forest by forest and not every forest is in it, so a genuinely empty answer for an area outside any published forest is not an error. If you want the base map regardless, turn the MVUM layer off explicitly and build without it — the page will not quietly drop a layer you asked for.

**A layer says "partial" instead of "complete."** The map services answer a large rectangle one page at a time, and say in the answer whether more is waiting. A layer reads "partial" when the service still had more than it handed over, so what the page holds is a piece of that layer, not all of it. The build refuses on a partial layer for the same reason it refuses a missing tile: a truncated set is worse than none, because on paper it looks finished. Draw a smaller box and check sources again. Recreation sites and trails used to report themselves ready in exactly this situation — the sheet printed with trails missing and nothing said so.

**"Named terrain (GNIS) must load completely before export."** The named-terrain query for your area came back partial, over the record cap, or timed out. The build refuses rather than print a set of place names with unstated gaps. Try again, draw a smaller box, or switch the names layer off explicitly to build without it.

**The build is slow, or the tab runs out of memory.** Every tile and every sheet is rendered in your browser, so a large area at 300 dpi is real work. The page shows an estimate before you start. If it is uncomfortable, cut the area, drop to 200 dpi, or use 1:48,000.

**Nothing downloads at the end.** PDF creation targets desktop Chromium and Edge first. A browser that blocks large blob downloads, or a private window with storage disabled, can stop the file being handed over.

**The area came back slightly different after a reload.** Recipes saved before September 2026 stored only the projected sheet extent, so restoring one rebuilds the box from the sheet grid and returns a slightly larger area. Redraw the box once and it will save and restore exactly from then on.

**I removed a quadrangle and the page count did not change.** A sheet prints wherever any selected quadrangle reaches it, so a quadrangle whose ground sits on sheets its neighbours also need saves nothing. The list says so before you remove it.

**I pressed Clear print area and now there is no area after a reload.** That is expected — Clear print area removes the saved recipe along with the area (and a shared link's area settings from the address bar), so there is nothing left to restore, even if you reload the instant after pressing it. Undo brings back exactly what Clear removed, but only until you leave the page; after that, draw a new area.
