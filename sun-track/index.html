sun-track is a Python library and command-line tool with two parts. The first estimates how high the sun is above the horizon at a latitude, longitude and UTC time, and reports whether that elevation falls in a band it calls golden hour. The second looks up where a GPX track log put you at a given time, so a photo with a timestamp but no GPS coordinates can still get a position. Install it with pip install git+https://github.com/Back-Road-Creative/sun-track. It needs Python 3.11 or newer, has no runtime dependencies and makes no network calls.

Estimating the sun’s position

solar_elevation_deg() returns the sun’s apparent elevation in degrees: positive above the horizon, negative below. It uses the NOAA solar-position algorithm (equation of time, declination, hour angle) plus a standard atmospheric-refraction correction, so the result is where the sun appears to be rather than the purely geometric angle. It returns elevation only. There is no azimuth, no sunrise, sunset or twilight times, and no moon.

How it classifies golden hour

The tool has one photographic period: golden hour, defined as an apparent elevation from −4° to +6°, both edges included. is_golden_elevation() tests a number against that band and is_golden_at() does both steps from a position and a time. The edges are a photographic judgment, not a physical constant: −4° reaches into the afterglow just after sunset, and +6° is roughly where the light stops reading as warm. They are defined in one place in the source (GOLDEN_ELEVATION_MIN and GOLDEN_ELEVATION_MAX), and nothing else in the library depends on those values.

Elevation is a useful stand-in because a low sun shines through more atmosphere. It is only one input to how light looks. Clouds, haze, smoke and nearby terrain change the result, and a time inside the band is a calculated window, not a promise of warm light.

It is also why a fixed clock window does not work. At 55° north on the Greenwich meridian the morning band runs 07:49–09:22 UTC in mid-January and 02:46–04:20 UTC at the June solstice. A fixed 07:00–08:00 rule overlaps the January band by about eleven minutes and misses the June one. Moving 15° east shifts the June band to 01:46–03:20, exactly an hour earlier (four minutes per degree of longitude). On the equator at the June solstice the band is about 43 minutes long, because the sun crosses those elevations more steeply.

Accuracy

The declination and equation-of-time terms are Fourier fits in day-of-year, not a full ephemeris. The project documents the worst-case error as about half a degree of apparent elevation. The error is a few hundredths of a degree near the solstices and a few tenths near the equinoxes, because the sun’s declination changes fastest then. The fits depend only on the calendar date, so a given date gives the same answer in every non-leap year while the real sun drifts about a quarter of a day per year within the leap cycle.

The repository’s tests are the evidence for that figure, and they cover a limited range. They check the day’s peak elevation at longitude 0 in 2026: within 0.1° at the solstices (latitudes 0°, ±35°, 53° and 66.5°) and within 0.6° at the equinoxes (10°, 53° and −40°), where part of that slack comes from the reference value rather than the code. Other checks cover the equation of time, the four-minutes-per-degree longitude shift, and polar day and night. Treat half a degree as the author’s estimate within that range. It is good enough for a ten-degree band and not for pointing a telescope; for arc-seconds, use an ephemeris library.

Other limits:

  • Precision falls off within a degree or so of straight overhead, which only matters in the tropics near local noon.
  • Refraction assumes a standard atmosphere. Within a degree or so of the horizon, real refraction varies by a few tenths of a degree with temperature and pressure.
  • There is no terrain and no observer altitude. A ridge to the west puts the sun out of sight before the computed elevation reaches zero.

Input and time zones

Every entry point needs a timezone-aware datetime; any aware value is converted to UTC. A naive datetime raises ValueError instead of being guessed. Reading a camera’s local clock time as UTC is the largest error source here: an hour of error is roughly 10 to 18 degrees of elevation at mid-latitudes, as large as or larger than the whole ten-degree golden band. The command line is more lenient: it takes ISO-8601 times, and one with no offset is read as UTC, as the GPX format specifies for its own timestamps. Convert a camera’s local time yourself before passing it in.

Matching timestamps to track positions

load_track_index() reads every *.gpx file directly inside a directory (not subdirectories) and merges them into one time-sorted index. Only <trkpt> points with a <time> and valid lat and lon are indexed; waypoints, routes and untimed points are ignored. A file that cannot be parsed is skipped with a warning, and a malformed point is dropped on its own.

TrackIndex.position_at() linearly interpolates between the two points on either side of the time you give it. The interpolation is a straight line in latitude and longitude, not a path along a road or a great circle, with longitude taken the short way across the date line. The result is a plausible position, not a fix, and fix_at() returns the same position with an inferred flag that is false only when the logger recorded a point at exactly that instant.

How wide a gap is accepted depends on the policy:

  • Default: the position is returned when the nearer of the two bracketing points is within max_gap_min minutes, 90 by default. A capture just after a point that precedes a three-hour hole is still placed, by interpolating across the whole hole. Before the first point or after the last, it returns that end point’s own position if it is within the window. It never extrapolates.
  • strict=True: max_gap_min limits the span between the two bracketing points, so a capture inside a wider hole is refused wherever it falls, and a capture before the first or after the last point is refused. Use it when a position will be shown as evidence of where the camera was.

Both return None when nothing qualifies. There is no filtering on reported GPS accuracy or speed. From the shell, python -m sun_track where <dir> <time> [max-gap-minutes] prints lat,lon or NONE (exit 1); it uses the default policy only.

Using the two together

The parts compose but neither imports the other: take a position from the track, then ask for the elevation there. A kilometre of position error moves the elevation by about a hundredth of a degree, according to the library’s own notes, so a coarse track position is usually enough.

1position = index.position_at(capture_utc)
2golden = position is not None and is_golden_at(position[0], position[1], capture_utc)
